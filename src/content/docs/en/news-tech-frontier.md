## What "Frontier" Means Here

Frontier is a noun and a stance. The Science Frontier column only picks things worth reading
further: research worth explaining carefully, tech shifts with public value, and science anyone
can discuss. We'd rather post fewer, better pieces than chase a daily feed.

Unlike a news ticker, each post gives context — why it matters, what researchers are solving, and
what it implies for the rest of us. If parts are hard to follow, that's expected; explaining is
basically the point.

## How a Column Article Comes Together

We roughly follow this order:

1. **Background**: where the question comes from and what earlier work established.
2. **What is new**: what actually happened this time, compared with previous work.
3. **Why it matters**: which research, applications, or public discussions it may affect.
4. **Limits**: sample size, conditions, disputes, and open questions.
5. **Further reading**: the original paper, official material, or reliable background.

Not every piece can go equally deep, but readers should at least know how firm the conclusion is.

## What Review Checks

Topics first pass our editor notes, then a review pass checks the writing is clear and the claims
keep their limits — never selling "maybe" as "certain."

Review is less about making the prose sound sophisticated than about checking:

- Whether key concepts are explained rather than assumed.
- Whether figures, quotes, and conclusions match the original sources.
- Whether correlation is being written as causation, or a lab result as a general rule.
- Whether the headline promises something the body does not answer.

## How We Pick & Improve

The column updates on a loose schedule, and we hope it isn't one-way. If you see something other
Phientist members should read, forward it. A single link is enough — leave the unpacking to us.

We do not choose by trending rank alone, but by whether something is worth explaining. Some hot
topics fade in two days; some quiet questions deserve repeated visits. We prefer the latter.

## Corrections and Discussion

If an article contains a factual error, we correct it and note the change at the end. If you
simply disagree with a judgment, the community is the place to continue the discussion. Science
should expect to be questioned — asking is more valuable than pretending to understand.
